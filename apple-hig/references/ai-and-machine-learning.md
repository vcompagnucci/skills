# AI and machine learning

How to design features built on generative AI and machine learning. Apple's position: use AI only where it adds clear value, keep people in control, say when AI is involved, and design for its mistakes, its latency, and models that change. (`generative-ai`, `machine-learning`)

**Contents:** When to use AI · Generative AI · Machine learning · Key source articles

## When to use AI

- **Offer generative AI only where it adds clear, specific value.** Time savings, better communication, or more creativity justify it. When AI only complements the core, like Genmoji beside regular emoji, offer a non-AI fallback, since features can be unavailable or declined. (`generative-ai`)

## Generative AI (`generative-ai`)

- **Offer generative features only when and where they provide clear, specific value, like time savings, better communication, or more creativity.** Generative AI isn't the right solution for every situation. It can help people edit text, create stories and images, or interact with a game character through AI-generated dialog. (`generative-ai`)
- **Ensure a great experience even when generative features aren't available or people opt not to use them.** Where AI only complements the core, offer a non-AI fallback when possible, as people can use regular emoji instead of Genmoji, or read notifications without Apple Intelligence summaries. (`generative-ai`)
- **Design your experience responsibly, considering its direct and indirect impacts on people, systems, and society.** Prototyping is easy but robustness is hard: the same input can produce very different outcomes, and you can't anticipate every request. Aim for experiences that are inclusive, designed with care, and protect privacy. (`generative-ai`)
- **Keep people in control of decision making and the overall experience.** Honor requests that are in scope with a clear expected output, and handle sensitive content carefully. Let people dismiss new content they don't want, and revert or retry transformations or other actions they disagree with. (`generative-ai`)
- **Ensure an inclusive experience: when depicting people, ask for the information you need instead of inferring personal or cultural traits.** Models favor the most common information, which breeds harmful biases and stereotypes, so seek clarity before assuming gender identity or relationship type, and test with diverse people. (`generative-ai`)
- **Communicate when and where your app uses AI, and never trick people into thinking they're interacting with a human.** Disclosure sets expectations and lets people knowingly choose an AI feature. Never present AI-authored content as a person's work, and align disclosure with each region's regulations. (`generative-ai`)
- **Set clear expectations about what your AI feature can and can't do.** It helps people build a mental model. Introduce the feature with a brief tutorial, offer curated suggestions in an open-ended search bar or prompt, and state known limitations up front with why inferior results occur. (`generative-ai`)
- **Choose an on-device or server model by what the feature needs, always weighing privacy alongside capability and performance.** On-device models keep people's information on the device, respond quickly, and work offline. Server-based models are worth considering when a feature needs more processing power or a larger context size. (`generative-ai`)
- **Protect privacy in server-based processing: process as much as possible locally, and minimize what's shared.** Be transparent: tell people their information may be sent to a server, show them what's shared, and help them understand what may be stored off-device or used for training. (`generative-ai`)
- **Ask permission before using personal information and usage data, then use the minimum and always offer a clear opt-out.** AI interactions can involve personal details, messages, photos, and usage data. Get explicit permission to store sensitive data or use it for model improvement, and handle it with care. (`generative-ai`)
- **Understand the privacy approach of any third party you share data with.** Also remember that model outputs can inadvertently contain sensitive information, and that apps for kids have stricter rules and laws around what data you can use. (`generative-ai`)
- **Clearly disclose how your app and its model use and store personal information, including whether it's used for training.** People share data more comfortably when they understand how it's used. When asking permission, explain the benefits concisely and specifically, so people can make an informed decision. (`generative-ai`)
- **Thoughtfully evaluate model capabilities, with a hands-on look at the available models and data as early as you can.** Some models have general knowledge while others are trained for specific tasks, so understanding a model's capabilities helps orient your design. (`generative-ai`)
- **Expect some model types to be unavailable to people because of device compatibility, network access, or battery level.** The Foundation Models framework, for example, requires a compatible device with Apple Intelligence turned on. (`generative-ai`)
- **Be intentional when choosing or creating a dataset, since the data greatly shapes the model's behavior.** Learn where data comes from and how it was gathered, license all data you don't own, and offer appropriate choices when you use people's data. (`generative-ai`)
- **Teach and evaluate the model with datasets covering a diverse range of subject matter.** Most real-world datasets are imperfect, so allow time for testing and evaluation that proactively mitigates bias and misinformation a model might learn and replicate. (`generative-ai`)
- **Guide people on how to use your generative feature, with diverse, predefined example inputs that hint at what's possible.** Steering and educating people this way helps them produce great results. (`generative-ai`)
- **Raise awareness of hallucinations by clearly communicating that AI-generated content may contain errors.** A model unsure how to respond can produce plausible, made-up content and present it convincingly as fact, and generative models sometimes get details wrong, like dates of important events or information about people. (`generative-ai`)
- **Scope what you ask a model to generate, to keep hallucinations rare.** Don't request facts unless the model has verified, current data for the task, and keep AI-generated content out of any place where a made-up answer could mislead and harm someone. (`generative-ai`)
- **Get permission before irreversible or potentially problematic tasks, and avoid automating destructive or hard-to-undo actions.** Ask whether a mistake or an irreversible action would cause people more work or stress, as deleting photos or making a purchase could, and generally confirm significant actions taken on someone's behalf. (`generative-ai`)
- **Review and follow model-specific usage policies and the government AI regulations of each locale where your generative features will be available.** Some situations may be prohibited or carry extra rules. (`generative-ai`)
- **Put controls like Edit, Undo, Retry, or Adjust near generated content, and acknowledge when a correction takes effect.** They let people refine or revert results while still benefiting from automation, and a clear signal that an adjustment worked builds an accurate mental model and trust over time. (`generative-ai`)
- **Help people improve requests when output is blocked or undesirable, coaching them to succeed next time.** If asked for harmful content, Image Playground says it's "Unable to use that description." Where possible, offer example requests that might lead to better results. (`generative-ai`)
- **Reduce unexpected and harmful outcomes with thoughtful design and thorough testing.** Most people mean well, but harm can still come from accidental or purposeful misuse, or from sensitive topics. Identify risks, devise policies for them, and use what you learn to improve the model, inform prevention, and respond thoughtfully. (`generative-ai`)
- **Challenge your policies and expected use cases by testing out-of-scope requests and ones poorly represented in training data.** Also try requests that are poorly phrased or ambiguous, that raise personal, sensitive, or controversial topics, or that push for harmful or incorrect results. (`generative-ai`)
- **Strive to avoid replicating copyrighted content.** Models trained on vast internet datasets can unintentionally produce content similar to published work. Build on models that already protect against this, curate inputs, like a set of pre-approved prompts, or explicitly tell the model to avoid mimicking certain content or styles. (`generative-ai`)
- **Factor processing time into your design: show a loading experience, or generate in the background while people use another part of the app.** Generative models typically take longer to produce output than non-generative ones, like body position tracking, which have low latency and can run in real time. (`generative-ai`)
- **Give specific, reassuring feedback during generation, and explain failures plainly with a clear next step.** Describing what's actually happening, like "Finding substitutions for ingredients" or "Summarizing key themes from your notes" instead of "Processing…", reduces uncertainty and makes waiting feel purposeful. (`generative-ai`)
- **Consider offering alternate versions of results, if several meaningfully different ones suit the feature better than one.** Choice gives people more control and bridges the gap between the model's interpretation and what they want: Image Playground generates multiple images of a person to pick from. (`generative-ai`)
- **Consider ways to improve your model over time, adapting to behavior, feedback, new data, and new capabilities.** Update some things, like a list of blocked words, frequently and independently of app releases, and plan significant improvements and new features around regular app updates. (`generative-ai`)
- **When moving to a newer, more capable base model, plan for fine-tuning, retesting, and prompt engineering.** If you train your own model, retrain with additional data and fine-tune it, and thoroughly test and refine every model update to catch unexpected behavior. (`generative-ai`)
- **Let people share feedback on outputs, and always make it voluntary.** Feedback reveals unexpected outcomes and issues that testing missed, and lets people celebrate what they like or report outputs that miss their expectations. Build trust by taking feedback seriously and resolving issues quickly. (`generative-ai`)
- **Place the feedback affordance in a clear location that doesn't interrupt the experience.** Respect people's time with a quick way to give positive and negative feedback, like thumbs-up and thumbs-down buttons, and consider a way to share detailed feedback on complicated issues. (`generative-ai`)
- **Design flexible, adaptable features, separating the model from the experience so you can swap in other models over time.** Generative AI advances rapidly and models' resource needs keep changing, so lay a foundation that allows such adjustments while still offering the same great experience. (`generative-ai`)

## Machine learning (`machine-learning`)

- **Design models with the intended app experience in mind, and be ready to change how you use data and metrics if the experience changes.** Adjusting model behavior can take a long time, and features like curated feeds depend on models as much as on the UI. (`machine-learning`)
- **Design the experience by teaching the app how to interpret data and react, not by scripting reactions to fixed scenarios.** A machine learning app's behavior depends on the data it receives, reacting to changing information and conditions. (`machine-learning`)
- **First define the role machine learning plays in each feature, then use it to decide how the app receives and displays data.** Consider five areas: critical or complementary, private or public, proactive or reactive, visible or invisible, and dynamic or static. (`machine-learning`)
- **Decide whether machine learning is critical or complementary: complementary if the app still works without the feature.** The keyboard still works without QuickType suggestions, a complementary use, while Face ID couldn't work without machine learning. (`machine-learning`)
- **Expect people to demand more accuracy the more central a feature is to your app's purpose.** People expect a news app's personalized feed to be accurate and reliable, but they're often more forgiving when a secondary feature isn't perfect every time. (`machine-learning`)
- **Prioritize accuracy and reliability more as a feature's data gets more private and sensitive.** A health app that wrongly recommends a doctor visit causes anxiety and lost trust, while a wrong artist from a music app is inconsequential. Whatever the data, every app must protect privacy at all times. (`machine-learning`)
- **Expect less tolerance for low-quality results from proactive features than from reactive ones.** Proactive features give results nobody requested, like Siri Suggestions proposing a shortcut, while reactive ones, like QuickType, respond to what people do. Proactive ones may need extra data to avoid seeming intrusive or irrelevant. (`machine-learning`)
- **Expect an invisible feature to have a harder time showing its reliability and getting feedback, since people may not know it exists.** People judge a visible feature, like Image Playground, as they choose from its results, but many don't notice News suggesting topics and channels from past engagement. (`machine-learning`)
- **Know whether a model improves dynamically as people interact, or offline, changing the feature only when the app updates.** The choice shapes more than update frequency: dynamic features often incorporate calibration and explicit or implicit feedback, whereas static ones might not. (`machine-learning`)
- **Treat explicit feedback as information people provide in response to a specific request from your app.** It gives actionable information for improving content and the experience, like a menu offering "Love" or "Suggest Less Like This", while implicit feedback is what the app gleans from people's actions. (`machine-learning`)
- **Count favoriting and social feedback as implicit feedback, not explicit.** People favorite items for quick access and express emotions toward others to reach their own goals, and these tools don't support app-specific requests, though apps can still learn from them. (`machine-learning`)
- **Request explicit feedback only when necessary, and always make providing it voluntary.** People must take action to give it, so prefer implicit feedback, which needs no extra work. Communicate that explicit feedback can improve the experience without making it feel mandatory. (`machine-learning`)
- **Use simple, direct language to describe each explicit feedback option and its consequences.** "Suggest less pop music", "Suggest more thrillers", and "Mute politics for a week" say what happens, while an imprecise term like "dislike" doesn't convey consequences and can be hard to translate. (`machine-learning`)
- **Add an icon to an option description if it helps people understand it, but avoid using an icon by itself.** An icon can clarify or emphasize part of a description, but alone it may not communicate the option's granularity or consequences. (`machine-learning`)
- **When asking for explicit feedback, consider several options that get more specific.** Choices give people a sense of control and help them flag and remove suggestions they don't want, and the narrower options let them clarify what was wrong. (`machine-learning`)
- **Act immediately on explicit feedback and persist the resulting changes.** If people identify content they don't want to see, hide it and make sure it doesn't appear elsewhere in your app. Reacting at once and showing that the app remembers builds trust in the value of giving feedback. (`machine-learning`)
- **Consider using explicit feedback to improve when and where you show results.** People might like a result but not want to see it at certain times or in certain contexts, so feedback on timing and placement helps you fine-tune the experience. (`machine-learning`)
- **Consider implicit feedback, the information that arises as people use your features, to improve the experience without extra work for them.** It reveals a wide range of behavior and preferences, unlike explicit feedback's specific responses, though a great machine learning app doesn't require it. (`machine-learning`)
- **Always secure the information implicit feedback gathers, with strict controls on privacy.** Implicit feedback can collect potentially sensitive information about people, so take particular care to protect it. (`machine-learning`)
- **Help people control their information: tell them how your app gets and shares it, and give them ways to restrict its flow.** People may be surprised when what they do in one app affects another, and may assume apps share their private information, which costs their trust. (`machine-learning`)
- **Don't let implicit feedback decrease people's opportunities to explore, and beware of confirmation bias.** Implicit feedback reinforces current behavior, which can help short term but hurt long term, and it reflects only what people can already see and do, so avoid relying on it alone to inform results. (`machine-learning`)
- **When possible, use multiple feedback signals to improve suggestions and mitigate mistakes.** Implicit feedback is indirect: viewing a photo, sharing it in a message, and adding it to a shared album doesn't necessarily mean someone likes it. Combine sources and interactions to avoid misreading intent. (`machine-learning`)
- **Consider withholding private or sensitive suggestions.** People often share accounts and devices with others, or switch from a personal device to a communal one, so avoid recommendations based on implicit feedback about private or sensitive topics. (`machine-learning`)
- **Prioritize recent feedback, falling back to historical feedback when recent feedback isn't available.** Tastes change frequently, and Face ID prioritizes recent facial input because it most likely represents how the person looks now. (`machine-learning`)
- **Update predictions on a cadence that matches people's mental model of the feature.** People expect typing suggestions to update immediately as they type, but continuously updated song recommendations make individual songs hard to consider and can feel rushed or overwhelming. (`machine-learning`)
- **Be prepared for implicit feedback to change when you change your app's UI.** Even small UI changes can noticeably alter its amount and types: moving a button can change how people use it with no change in the benefit it offers. Account for this when interpreting implicit feedback. (`machine-learning`)
- **Use calibration, where people provide information a feature needs to function, only when the feature can't work without it.** To use Face ID, people must first scan their face. Otherwise, gather what you need through implicit feedback, or possibly explicit feedback. (`machine-learning`)
- **Always secure the information people provide during calibration, and collect only the most essential.** People may provide sensitive information, and requesting a minimal amount makes them more comfortable participating and increases their trust in your app. (`machine-learning`)
- **Be clear about why you need people's information, briefly emphasizing what the feature does rather than how it works.** Calibration usually comes before people can use a feature, so they need to understand the value of providing their information. (`machine-learning`)
- **Avoid asking people to calibrate more than once, and calibrate early in the experience.** Afterward, evolve what you know through implicit or explicit feedback. The exception is calibrating with an object, not a person: an app that improves people's baseball swing might calibrate with each new field. (`machine-learning`)
- **Make calibration quick and easy: get a few important pieces of information and infer the rest from other sources or feedback.** Even brief calibration takes time and effort, so avoid asking for information most people would have to look up, or for actions that might be difficult. (`machine-learning`)
- **Give calibration an explicit goal and show progress toward it.** Face ID tells people briefly what to do, and the tick marks around the face change as the scan advances. (`machine-learning`)
- **Immediately provide assistance if calibration progress stalls, with actionable recommendations and a clear next step.** A stall can make people feel stuck or powerless and cost their trust. Never imply that something's wrong or that people are at fault. (`machine-learning`)
- **Confirm success the moment calibration completes, with a clear path toward using the feature.** An explicit completion rewards people's time and effort, reinforces the unique nature of the experience, and helps them switch their focus to your feature. (`machine-learning`)
- **Let people cancel calibration easily at any time, without implying judgment or showing a message about it.** The next time people try to use the feature, they'll get another chance to participate. (`machine-learning`)
- **Give people a way to update or remove information they provided during calibration, including outside the calibration experience.** Editing their information gives people more control and trust, and access outside calibration lets them feel free to make changes at any time. (`machine-learning`)
- **Plan for mistakes: anticipate them, help people handle them, and learn from them when that improves the app.** Mistakes are inevitable and can decrease trust, so design ways to avoid and mitigate them, and tools that match their consequences. Learning from one can sometimes make the experience unpredictable. (`machine-learning`)
- **Address mistakes with five patterns: limitations, corrections, attribution, confidence, and feedback.** Limitations set expectations about accuracy, corrections let people succeed even when results are wrong, attribution shows where suggestions come from, confidence gauges result quality, and explicit and implicit feedback reveal mistakes you might miss. (`machine-learning`)
- **Understand the significance of a mistake's consequences, and show empathy with corrective tools that match its seriousness.** Incorrect keyboard suggestions might annoy people, but a suggested travel route that makes them miss a flight is a serious inconvenience. (`machine-learning`)
- **Make it easy for people to correct frequent or predictable mistakes.** Without an easy way to fix mistakes, people can lose trust in your app. (`machine-learning`)
- **Continuously update the feature to reflect people's evolving interests and preferences, and to help avoid mistakes.** Use implicit feedback to discover changes in tastes and habits, and add domain-specific information, like current trends in popular entertainment. Ideally, people benefit from improvements without doing any work. (`machine-learning`)
- **When possible, address mistakes without complicating the UI, weighing each pattern's effect on the UI against its risk of compounding the mistake.** Corrections and limitations tend to fit into the UI easily, but attributions are harder: an attribution that turns out to be wrong magnifies the original mistake. (`machine-learning`)
- **Be especially careful to avoid mistakes in proactive features.** People don't request a proactive feature, like a suggestion based on their behavior, so they have less patience with its mistakes, which can also make them feel they have less control. (`machine-learning`)
- **As you reduce mistakes in one area, consider the effect on other areas and on overall accuracy.** Improving how an image-recognition app recognizes dogs might reduce its ability to recognize cats. Use people's preferences to choose what to work on, and expect mistakes to evolve with your models. (`machine-learning`)
- **Give people familiar, easy ways to make corrections by showing the steps your app takes as it automates a task.** Photos highlights the controls it used to auto-crop a photo, so people aren't confused and can refine or undo the result with those same controls. (`machine-learning`)
- **Provide immediate value when people make a correction, displaying the corrected content instantly and persisting it.** Instant results reward people's effort, especially in a critical feature or in response to direct input, and persisting spares people from making the same correction again. (`machine-learning`)
- **Let people correct their corrections, responding immediately and persisting the update.** People sometimes make a correction without realizing they've made a mistake, so treat an updated correction like any other. (`machine-learning`)
- **Always balance a feature's benefits with the effort a correction takes, and never rely on corrections to make up for low-quality results.** People may not mind an automated mistake but abandon a feature when doing the task themselves seems easier, and depending on corrections erodes trust and value. (`machine-learning`)
- **Learn from corrections when it makes sense.** A correction is implicit feedback that shows where your app misses people's expectations, but before using one to update your models, make sure it will lead to higher-quality results. (`machine-learning`)
- **When possible, use guided corrections, which suggest specific alternatives, instead of freeform corrections.** Guided ones need less effort, like a speech-to-text feature listing alternative completions, while freeform ones need more input, like adjusting a Photos auto-crop as people see fit. You can support a combination of both. (`machine-learning`)
- **Decide whether a feature presents a single result or multiple options.** Multiple options give people a greater sense of control, bridge the gap between your model's predictions and what people want, and encourage realistic expectations about the types of results your app generates. (`machine-learning`)
- **Know the three contexts for multiple options: suggested options, requested options, and corrections.** Suggested options are proactive, based on past interactions, like For You in Apple Music. Requested options react to recent actions, like QuickType suggestions. Corrections fix the app's mistakes when it acts for people, like Photos Auto-Crop. (`machine-learning`)
- **Prefer diverse options, balancing the accuracy of a response with the diversity of multiple options.** Apple Maps generally suggests several routes, like one without tolls, a scenic one, or one using highways, which helps people choose what they prefer and can surface new things that interest them. (`machine-learning`)
- **In general, avoid providing too many options, and list them on one screen when possible.** People must evaluate each option before choosing, so more options mean more cognitive load, and one screen spares people scrolling to find the right one. (`machine-learning`)
- **List the most likely option first, and consider selecting it by default.** Rank options by confidence once you know how it correlates with result quality, and use context like the time of day or current location. A default selection lets people reach their goal without reading every option. (`machine-learning`)
- **Make options easy to distinguish and choose.** When options look similar, briefly describe each and highlight the differences, as in a routing app where people must choose quickly. When there are too many for one view, like content recommendations, group them into categories people can scan rapidly. (`machine-learning`)
- **Learn from selections when it makes sense and doesn't hurt the experience.** Every selection is implicit feedback you can use to refine the options and raise the chance of listing the most likely option first, since continuing to offer incorrect results erodes trust in your predictions. (`machine-learning`)
- **Consider computing confidence values if your model doesn't produce them and they can improve the experience.** Confidence measures how certain a result is, and not all models produce confidence values by default. (`machine-learning`)
- **Verify that confidence values correspond to result quality, and don't convey confidence until you know they do.** Higher confidence doesn't necessarily mean a higher-quality result, so review values at several confidence thresholds or compare them across versions of your app. (`machine-learning`)
- **Know what your confidence values mean before you decide how to present them.** People may forgive low-quality results from critical or complementary features, especially with an attribution or other context, but presenting low-quality results prominently is likely to erode trust in your app. (`machine-learning`)
- **In general, translate confidence values into concepts people already understand.** A bare "97% match" beside a suggested song doesn't help people choose, while an attribution naming the behavior, like "Because you listen to pop music", is more actionable. (`machine-learning`)
- **When attributions aren't helpful, rank or order results in a way that implies confidence levels.** If you must display confidence directly, consider semantic categories: a travel-price predictor can replace ranges of confidence numbers with "high chance" and "low chance" so people can understand and compare results. (`machine-learning`)
- **Where people expect statistical or numerical information, display confidence values that help them interpret results.** Weather predictions, sports statistics, and polling numbers often express the accuracy of their data as an interval or a percentage. (`machine-learning`)
- **Whenever possible, convey confidence as actionable suggestions that serve people's goals.** People using a lowest-price predictor want to optimize time and money, so "This is a good time to buy" or "Consider waiting for a better price" beats a percentage. (`machine-learning`)
- **Consider changing how you present results at different confidence thresholds.** When confidence is high, Photos' face recognition simply shows the photos containing a person, but when it's lower, Photos asks people to confirm the person before showing more. (`machine-learning`)
- **Avoid showing results when confidence is low, once you know confidence values correspond to result quality.** Poor results annoy people and cost trust, especially from a proactive feature's unbidden suggestions, so set a confidence threshold below which suggestions and proactive features offer nothing. (`machine-learning`)
- **Use attributions to express the basis or rationale for a result, without explaining exactly how the model works.** They add transparency and insight into results, as "Because you've read mysteries" does beside books suggested in the thrillers category. (`machine-learning`)
- **Know what an attribution should do before you add one.** It can nudge what people do in the app, soften the impact of mistakes, help them form a mental model of the feature, and build trust over time. (`machine-learning`)
- **Consider using attributions to help people distinguish among results.** In a set of multiple options, an attribution like "New books by authors you've read" lets people choose based on their understanding of the premise behind each option. (`machine-learning`)
- **Avoid attributions that are too specific or too general.** Overly specific ones make people work to interpret results and can feel like the app watches too closely, while overly general ones typically give no useful information and feel like the app isn't treating people as individuals. (`machine-learning`)
- **Keep attributions factual and based on objective analysis, never implying you understand or judge people's emotions, preferences, or beliefs.** An attribution should help people reason about a result, not provoke an emotional response: write "Because you've read nonfiction", not "Because you love nonfiction". (`machine-learning`)
- **In general, avoid technical or statistical jargon when presenting results.** Percentages, statistics, and other jargon rarely help people assess results, except when the result is itself statistical or technical, as in weather, sports, polling and election results, or scientific data. (`machine-learning`)
- **Identify where limitations affect the experience: things a feature can't do well, and things it can't do at all.** A mismatch with people's expectations makes a limitation seem like a defect. Set expectations before use, show how to get the best results during use, and explain inferior results. (`machine-learning`)
- **Help people establish realistic expectations, warning them up front about a rare limitation with serious effects.** Describe it in marketing materials or within the feature's context so people can decide how much to rely on the feature. For limitations without serious effects, attributions can set expectations. (`machine-learning`)
- **Demonstrate how to get the best results, for example with placeholder text that suggests input.** Without guidance, people may assume a feature does everything they want. Photos' search bar shows "Photos, People, Places…", and Photos describes how it scans the library to offer search suggestions. (`machine-learning`)
- **As people use a feature, give feedback on their actions that guides them toward a result without overwhelming them.** Memoji responds to current conditions and suggests improving results by adjusting the lighting or moving closer to the camera. (`machine-learning`)
- **Suggest alternative ways to accomplish the goal instead of showing no results.** It works only when you understand the goal well enough to suggest alternatives that make sense. (`machine-learning`)
- **Explain how limitations can cause unsatisfactory results, so the feature doesn't seem to work intermittently.** Ideally, the feature recognizes and describes the reasons for poor results so people can adjust their expectations, as Memoji says it doesn't work well in the dark with a "Low light" message. (`machine-learning`)
- **Consider telling people when you resolve a limitation.** Frequent users learn to avoid interactions that fail because of a limitation, so a notice after an update lets them adjust their mental model and return to interactions they'd avoided. (`machine-learning`)
