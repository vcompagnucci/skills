# Accessibility and inclusion

How to make an app work for everyone, whatever their abilities, language, culture, or reading direction. Apple's position: an accessible interface is intuitive, perceivable, and adaptable, so text enlarges, contrast holds, nothing depends on color or sound alone, every gesture has an alternative, and system settings like Reduce Motion are respected. And an inoffensive app isn't necessarily an inclusive one. (`accessibility`, `inclusion`)

## Accessibility (`accessibility`)

- **Audit accessibility as you design with Accessibility Inspector, and report it with Accessibility Nutrition Labels.** The inspector highlights issues and shows how your app represents itself to system accessibility features, and the labels tell people on the App Store how accessible your app is. (`accessibility`)
- **Let people enlarge text by at least 200 percent.** Adjustable text and icons are more legible, visible, and comfortable to read, so adopt Dynamic Type, the systemwide text-size setting, or support enlargement in your custom UI. (`accessibility`)
- **Follow each platform's default and minimum sizes for custom type styles, and go larger with thin weights.** Default / minimum is 17 / 11 pt in iOS and iPadOS and 13 / 10 pt in macOS, and a thin custom font needs more than the recommended size to stay legible. (`accessibility`)
- **Meet the minimum contrast ratios: 4.5:1 for text up to 17 pt, and 3:1 for text of 18 pt or larger and for bold text at any size.** These are the WCAG Level AA values Accessibility Inspector checks, in both light and dark appearances. WCAG and APCA are two popular standards to check against. (`accessibility`)
- **If your default colors can't meet those ratios, provide a higher-contrast scheme when Increase Contrast is on, and prefer system colors.** System-defined colors have accessible variants that adapt automatically to Increase Contrast and to light and dark appearances. (`accessibility`)
- **Never convey information with color alone.** People with color blindness struggle with pairings like red-green and blue-orange, so add distinct shapes or icons for differences in function and state, and consider letting people customize colors, as in charts or game characters. (`accessibility`)
- **Describe your interface and content for VoiceOver.** The screen reader lets people use the app without seeing the display, so it depends on the descriptions you provide. (`accessibility`)
- **Never convey dialogue or important information through audio alone.** Depending on the context, offer text-based ways to experience audio and video, like captions, subtitles, audio descriptions, or transcripts, and let people customize how that text looks. (`accessibility`)
- **Match the text alternative to the media.** Captions sync text with audible information, as in game cutscenes and video clips, subtitles show dialogue in people's preferred language for TV and movies, audio descriptions narrate visual-only information in pauses, and transcripts suit podcasts and audiobooks. (`accessibility`)
- **Pair audio cues with matching haptics.** A success chime, error sound, or game feedback then reaches people who can't hear it or have audio off. In iOS and iPadOS, Music Haptics and Audio Graphs let people experience music and infographics through vibration and texture. (`accessibility`)
- **Augment audio cues with visual cues, especially in games.** Important content may happen offscreen, so when audio guides people toward an action, add visual indicators that point to where you want them to interact. (`accessibility`)
- **Meet each platform's control size, and never go below its minimum.** Default / minimum is 44x44 / 28x28 pt in iOS and iPadOS and 28x28 / 20x20 pt in macOS. Controls that are too small are hard for many people to select. (`accessibility`)
- **Treat spacing between controls as importantly as their size.** Tight spacing makes people hit the wrong control, so leave about 12 pt of padding around an element with a bezel and about 24 pt around the visible edges of one without. (`accessibility`)
- **Use the simplest gesture possible for frequent interactions.** Complex gestures challenge many people, with or without disabilities, so avoid custom multifinger and multihand gestures and keep repetitive actions comfortable and easy to remember. (`accessibility`)
- **Give every gesture an onscreen alternative.** Gestures can be uncomfortable for people with limited dexterity, so make core functionality reachable more than one way: back a swipe-to-dismiss with a button people can tap or use with an assistive device. (`accessibility`)
- **Support Voice Control by labeling interface elements appropriately.** With it, people operate their device entirely by speaking, performing gestures, interacting with screen elements, and dictating and editing text. (`accessibility`)
- **Support Siri and Shortcuts so people can perform tasks by voice alone.** People automate important, repetitive tasks and start them from Siri, the Action button on iPhone, shortcuts on the Home Screen, or Control Center. (`accessibility`)
- **Test with mobility assistive technologies, and label elements for them.** VoiceOver, AssistiveTouch, Full Keyboard Access, Pointer Control, and Switch Control give people with low mobility other ways to interact, so verify your app supports each one. (`accessibility`)
- **Make the app usable with the keyboard alone, and never override system-defined keyboard shortcuts.** People navigate with Full Keyboard Access and rely on accessibility and other system shortcuts, so evaluate that your app works well with it. (`accessibility`)
- **Support Switch Control.** It lets people control their device through separate hardware, game controllers, or sounds like a click or a pop, selecting, tapping, typing, and drawing when your app supports navigation with it. (`accessibility`)
- **Keep actions simple and intuitive.** Use easy-to-remember, consistent interactions, and prefer system gestures and behaviors people already know to custom gestures they must learn and retain. (`accessibility`)
- **Minimize views and controls that dismiss on a timer, and prefer dismissal by an explicit action.** Timers penalize people who need longer to process information or whose assistive technology takes longer to move through the interface. (`accessibility`)
- **Consider difficulty accommodations in games.** To support a variety of cognitive abilities, let people reduce the criteria for completing a level, adjust reaction time, or turn on control assistance. (`accessibility`)
- **Never autoplay audio or video without discoverable controls to start and stop it.** Make those controls easy to act on, and consider a global setting that lets people opt out of autoplaying all audio and video. (`accessibility`)
- **Respond to the Dim Flashing Lights setting in video playback.** People may want to avoid bright, frequent flashes, and the setting lets the system calculate, mitigate, and inform people about flashing lights in media. (`accessibility`)
- **When Reduce Motion is on, cut automatic and repetitive animation like zooming, scaling, and peripheral motion.** Excessive fast-moving or blinking animation distracts, causes dizziness, and can trigger epileptic episodes. (`accessibility`)
- **Under Reduce Motion, replace x-, y-, and z-axis transitions with fades, tighten springs to reduce bounce, and track animation directly to gestures.** Also avoid animating depth changes in z-axis layers, or into and out of blurs. (`accessibility`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Optimize your UI for Assistive Access.** It gives people with cognitive disabilities a streamlined version of your app, with a default layout and control presentation that reduce cognitive load, like Camera's large Photo, Video, and Back buttons. (`accessibility`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) When Assistive Access is on, keep the core and one interaction per screen.** Remove noncritical workflows and UI elements, break up multistep workflows, and always confirm twice before an action that's hard to recover from, like deleting a file. (`accessibility`)

## VoiceOver (`voiceover`)

- **Give every key element a descriptive alternative label, and keep it current.** VoiceOver speaks these labels, which aren't visible. System controls ship with generic labels, so replace them with ones that convey your app's function, label every custom element, and update labels as content changes. (`voiceover`)
- **Describe meaningful images, but only the information the image itself conveys.** Without a description people miss key images, and VoiceOver already reads the surrounding interface, like a nearby caption, so don't repeat it. (`voiceover`)
- **Exclude purely decorative images from VoiceOver.** Describing images that carry no useful or actionable information wastes people's time, and excluding them reduces cognitive load. (`voiceover`)
- **Describe relationships that are only visual, like how elements are grouped, ordered, or linked.** VoiceOver follows the language's reading order, so two ungrouped images are both read before their captions, while grouping each image with its caption reads them together. (`voiceover`)
- **Tell VoiceOver when visible content or layout changes.** An unannounced change leaves people's mental map of the content wrong and confusing, and reporting it lets assistive technologies help them update it. (`voiceover`)
- **Give each page or screen a unique, succinct title, and use accurate section headings.** The title is the first thing people hear on arrival, so it should describe the content and purpose, and headings convey the information hierarchy. (`voiceover`)
- **Make charts and infographics fully accessible, interactions included.** Give each a concise description of what it conveys, and if people can interact with it to get more information, make those interactions available through VoiceOver too. (`voiceover`)
- **Support the VoiceOver rotor where you can.** Identifying headings, links, and other content types lets people navigate by them, and the rotor can also bring up the braille keyboard. (`voiceover`)
- **Support VoiceOver in games as well as apps, including Unity games through Apple's Unity plug-ins.** It lets people who are blind or have low vision reach your information and navigate your content when they can't see the display. (`voiceover`)

## Inclusion (`inclusion`)

- **Look at your work from many perspectives, and aim past merely avoiding offense.** Depending on age, gender identity, race, disability, language, religion, education, or economic context, a word or image can be incomprehensible or mean something you didn't intend. Start by examining your own assumptions. (`inclusion`)
- **Address people as "you" and "your", never "the user" or "the player", and keep "we" for your app or company.** Indirect references feel distant, and "we" used otherwise implies a personal relationship that can read as condescending. (`inclusion`)
- **Keep the tone of your copy clear, direct, and respectful.** Style communicates almost as much as words, and an academic tone can signal that only highly educated people are welcome. (`inclusion`)
- **Define any specialized or technical term, or use plain language instead.** Jargon excludes people who don't know it, so define terms first and make definitions easy to look up. Plain sentences are also easier to read and translate. (`inclusion`)
- **Replace colloquial expressions with plain language.** They're culture-specific and hard to translate, and some, like "peanut gallery" and "grandfathered in", arose from oppressive contexts and still exclude people. (`inclusion`)
- **Think carefully before including humor.** It's subjective and hard to translate, so it can confuse people who don't get it, irritate people who tire of it, and insult people who read it differently. (`inclusion`)
- **Make the experience approachable: no prerequisite skills, a clear interface, and a built-in way to learn.** An onboarding flow can walk newcomers through step by step while letting others skip straight to the content. (`inclusion`)
- **Avoid unnecessary references to gender, and ask for it only when you must.** Write "Subscribers can post recipes to your shared folder", not "his or her recipes". When health or legal reasons require gender, offer nonbinary, self-identify, and decline-to-state options, plus pronouns. (`inclusion`)
- **Let people customize avatars, emoji, glyphs, and characters, and show a generic person with a nongendered image.** A generic person means human, not man or woman, and SF Symbols offers nongendered figure and person symbols. (`inclusion`)
- **Portray a range of people, activities, and settings, without stereotypes.** People who see others like themselves feel less excluded: a fitness app can show people of different races, body types, ages, and physical capabilities, and never only male doctors or female nurses. (`inclusion`)
- **Prefer places, homes, activities, and items that are familiar to most people.** Showing high affluence makes sense in some scenarios, but elsewhere it can feel unwelcoming and make an experience seem out of touch. (`inclusion`)
- **Don't build assumptions about people's lives into flows and copy.** A narrow definition of family excludes everyone else, and "What was the make of your first car?" excludes people without one, so ask "What was the name of your first friend?" (`inclusion`)
- **Support the accessibility features people rely on, and never assume a disability means someone won't want your experience.** People use features like VoiceOver, Display Accommodations, closed captioning, Switch Control, and Speak Screen. Each disability is a spectrum, and everyone meets temporary or situational ones. (`inclusion`)
- **Include people with disabilities when you portray a variety of people, and never use a disability to express a negative quality.** Images and language that leave people with disabilities out exclude them. (`inclusion`)
- **Write about disability people-first.** Describe someone's accomplishments and goals before a disability they may have, and when writing about a specific person or community, find out how they self-identify. (`inclusion`)
- **Prioritize simplicity and perceivability.** Prefer familiar, consistent interactions that make tasks simple to perform, and make sure everyone can perceive your content, whether they use sight, hearing, or touch. (`inclusion`)
- **Prepare for other languages and regions, and check what your colors mean in each locale.** Inclusive writing and SF Symbols ease localization, and white signals death or grief in some places and purity or peace in others, so color must say the same thing in every version. (`inclusion`)

## Right to left (`right-to-left`)

- **Start from system components and standard layouts, which flip automatically in a right-to-left context.** You may need no changes beyond fine-tuning layout details and locale-specific currencies, numerals, and mathematical symbols. (`right-to-left`)
- **Align text with the interface direction when the system doesn't do it for you.** Text left-aligned with content in a left-to-right context gets right-aligned to match the content's mirrored position. (`right-to-left`)
- **Align a paragraph of three or more lines by its own language, not the context.** Right-aligned left-to-right text hides where each line starts, so only one- and two-line text blocks follow the reading direction of the context. (`right-to-left`)
- **Use one alignment for every text item in a list.** Reverse the alignment of all the items, including those in a different script, so the list stays comfortable to read and scan. (`right-to-left`)
- **Never reverse the digits of a number, but reverse the order of numerals that show progress or sequence.** "541", phone numbers, and credit card numbers always read the same, while the numerals under a flipped slider or rating control swap order without flipping themselves. (`right-to-left`)
- **In a number-centric app, choose the number system per locale.** Hebrew uses Western Arabic numerals, while Arabic may use Western or Eastern Arabic numerals, varying by country and even within one. Other apps can rely on the system's representation. (`right-to-left`)
- **Flip controls that show progress from one value to another, and swap the glyphs at their ends.** People see forward progress as moving in their reading direction, so sliders and progress indicators flip. (`right-to-left`)
- **Flip controls that move through items in a fixed order.** A back button points right in a right-to-left context so the flow of screens matches the reading order, and next and previous buttons for an ordered list flip too. (`right-to-left`)
- **Keep the direction of a control that refers to a real direction or points to an onscreen area.** A control that means "to the right" always points right, whatever the context. (`right-to-left`)
- **Set Arabic or Hebrew text about 2 pt larger than adjacent all-caps Latin text.** These scripts have no uppercase letters, so in buttons, labels, and titles they look too small beside uppercased Latin. (`right-to-left`)
- **Don't flip photos, illustrations, or general artwork.** Flipping changes an image's meaning, and flipping a copyrighted image could be a violation. If an image's content is strongly tied to reading direction, create a new version instead. (`right-to-left`)
- **Reverse the positions of images whose order matters.** Images in chronological, alphabetical, or favorite order swap places to preserve that order's meaning. (`right-to-left`)
- **Use SF Symbols for interface icons to get right-to-left variants.** They include localized symbols for Arabic and Hebrew, among other languages, and you can specify the directionality of your custom symbols. (`right-to-left`)
- **Flip interface icons that represent text, reading direction, or forward and backward motion.** Bars standing for left-aligned text become right-aligned, and a speaker's sound waves come from the right, since motion in the reading direction reads as forward. (`right-to-left`)
- **Localize custom icons that show text, and drop text from icons whose concept isn't about reading or writing.** Letters help convey concepts like font size or a signature, and SF Symbols has Latin, Hebrew, and Arabic versions of such symbols. (`right-to-left`)
- **Never flip logos or universal marks, and generally don't flip icons of real-world objects.** A flipped logo confuses people and can have legal repercussions, a checkmark and a clock look the same everywhere, and a right-handed tool like a pencil stays as is. (`right-to-left`)
- **Before flipping a complex custom icon, weigh each component and the overall balance.** SF Symbols keeps the same negation backslash in both directions, a badge depicting real UI flips with it, and a plus badge may move to balance a flipped cart. A tool keeps its handedness. (`right-to-left`)
