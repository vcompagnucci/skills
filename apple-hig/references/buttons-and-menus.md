# Buttons and menus

How people trigger actions and choose options. For Apple, a button is instantly recognizable and easy to hit, with one prominent style for the likeliest action and never a destructive default, and menus stay familiar, short, and predictable, with every command also reachable somewhere visible (the main interface, or the menu bar on a Mac). (`buttons`, `menus`, `context-menus`, `the-menu-bar`)

## Choosing the control

- **Match the menu to its job.** A pop-up or pull-down button reveals options tied to its action, a context menu offers a few frequent actions for the current view or task, and in macOS and iPadOS the menu bar menus hold every command the app has. (`menus`)
- **Use a pop-up button for a flat list of mutually exclusive options or states.** The choice affects the content or the surrounding view. Use a pull-down button instead when you need a list of actions, multiple selection, or a submenu. (`pop-up-buttons`)
- **Use a pull-down button for commands or items directly related to the button's action.** Its menu clarifies the target or customizes the behavior without extra buttons: an Add button picks what to add, Sort the attribute, Back a specific location to revisit. (`pull-down-buttons`)
- **Balance a pull-down menu's length: at least three items, but not so many that people slow down.** People must open the button to see the menu, so fewer items don't feel worthwhile. For one or two, use buttons for actions and toggles or switches for selections. (`pull-down-buttons`)
- **Treat a context menu as a shortcut to a few item-specific commands, never the only path to them.** It's hidden by default, so repeat its items in the main interface, as Mail in iOS does in its message toolbar, and the macOS menu bar lists every command. (`context-menus`, `the-menu-bar`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Offer either a context menu or an edit menu for an item, never both.** Both on one item confuse people and make their intent hard for the system to detect. (`context-menus`)

## Buttons (`buttons`)

- **Build each button from a style, content, and a role.** Style is its size, color, and shape, content is a symbol or icon, a text label, or both, and the system-defined role states its meaning and can change its appearance. (`buttons`)
- **Make buttons easy to use: enough space around each one and a hit region of at least 44x44 pt.** Space sets a button apart from nearby components and content, and makes it easy to select with any input, whether a fingertip or a pointer. (`buttons`)
- **Use the system button styles to communicate a hierarchy of actions.** Each platform defines its own, and they allow customization while building in interaction states, accessibility support, and appearance adaptation. Toggles, pop-up buttons, and segmented controls are button-like components for specific uses. (`buttons`)
- **Always include a press state in a custom button.** Without one, the button feels unresponsive and people wonder whether it accepted their input. (`buttons`)
- **Use a prominent style for the most likely action, and keep to one or two prominent buttons per view.** The system puts the accent color behind a prominent button, colored buttons stand out most, and too many raise cognitive load as people weigh options. (`buttons`)
- **Distinguish the preferred option by style, not size.** Same-size buttons signal a coherent set of choices, while two sizes side by side look confusing and inconsistent, so give the preferred option the more prominent style. (`buttons`)
- **Don't tint button labels a color similar to the content layer behind them.** When the content is already bright and colorful, keep the default monochromatic label appearance. (`buttons`)
- **Make each button's purpose clear with a symbol or icon, a text label, or both.** In macOS, the system also shows a tooltip, a brief phrase explaining the button, after people hover over it for a moment. (`buttons`)
- **Pair familiar actions with familiar icons.** People predict that the square.and.arrow.up symbol shares. Consider an existing or customized SF Symbol, and the standard icons for common actions. (`buttons`)
- **Use text when a short label communicates more clearly than an icon.** Write a few words that succinctly describe the action, in title-style capitalization, and consider starting with a verb, like Add to Cart. (`buttons`)
- **Give a system button one of four roles: normal, primary, cancel, or destructive.** The role can change the button's look: the primary button, the one people are most likely to choose, uses the app's accent color, and a destructive one uses system red. (`buttons`)
- **Assign the primary role to the likeliest choice, but never to a destructive action, even the likeliest one.** The primary button answers Return and can close a sheet, editable view, or alert, and people sometimes choose a prominent button without reading it. (`buttons`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Show an activity indicator inside a button whose action doesn't finish instantly.** It saves space and explains the delay, and the label can change too, as Checkout becomes Checking out…, while the indicator hides any button image. (`buttons`)
- **(macOS) Start from the push button, the standard macOS button type.** It can show text, a symbol, an icon, an image, or text with an image, act as the default button in a view, and take a tint. (`buttons`)
- **(macOS) Use a flexible-height push button only for tall or variable-height content, like two lines of text or a tall icon.** It shares the regular push button's configurations, corner radius, and padding, so it stays consistent. Otherwise use a standard push button. (`buttons`)
- **(macOS) End a push button's label with an ellipsis when it opens another window, view, or app.** Across the system an ellipsis means people can give more input, like the Edit… buttons in Safari's AutoFill settings. (`buttons`)
- **(macOS) Consider supporting spring loading.** With a Magic Trackpad, people drag selected items over a button and force click to activate it without dropping them, then keep dragging to do more. (`buttons`)
- **(macOS) Use a square button for an action on a view, like adding or removing table rows, and place it within or beneath that view.** Being close to the view tells people which one it affects. It can act like a push button, toggle, or pop-up button. (`buttons`)
- **(macOS) Put a symbol in a square button, never text, and don't add a label to introduce it.** SF Symbols get the right coloring by default and on interaction, and the button's tie to its view already makes its purpose clear. (`buttons`)
- **(macOS) Keep square, help, and image buttons inside views, never in the window frame.** They aren't meant for toolbars or status bars, so use a toolbar item when you need a button or an image in a toolbar. (`buttons`)
- **(macOS) Use the system-provided help button, the circular one with a question mark, to open your help.** People know its look and that it opens help content, so don't add text introducing it. (`buttons`)
- **(macOS) Open the help topic for the current context, and use at most one help button per window.** Mail's Rules settings open that topic in the Mail User Guide, with the help's top level as the fallback. Several help buttons make the result of clicking one hard to predict. (`buttons`)
- **(macOS) Place a help button where people expect it.** In a dialog with dismissal buttons like OK and Cancel, put it in the lower corner opposite them, vertically aligned with them; in other dialogs and in settings windows or panes, use the lower-left or lower-right corner. (`buttons`)
- **(macOS) Pad an image button's image by about 10 pixels, skip the system border, and put any label below the button.** The button's edges define its clickable area even when they're invisible, so padding registers clicks that land slightly off the image. (`buttons`)

## Menus (`menus`)

- **Label each item clearly and succinctly, with a verb or verb phrase for actions, like View, Close, or Select.** Let the app's communication style set the tone, and include a symbol when it helps clarify the meaning. (`menus`)
- **Use title-style capitalization and drop articles like a, an, and the.** Capitalize every word except articles, coordinating conjunctions, and short prepositions, plus the last word. Articles lengthen labels without aiding understanding: View the Settings says no more than View Settings. (`menus`)
- **In an app, a menu item can also show its keyboard command, if it has one.** Games rarely do, because they handle input from more kinds of devices and may map keys their own way. (`menus`)
- **Dim unavailable items, but keep a menu openable even when all its items are unavailable.** Dimmed items don't respond, and people still need to open the menu to learn what commands it contains. (`menus`)
- **Append an ellipsis to an item that needs more information before it completes.** The … character signals that people must enter information or make more choices, usually in another view. (`menus`)
- **Represent common actions like Share, Print, and Search with the system's standard icons.** Standard icons make the app easier to use and more familiar. (`menus`)
- **Use menu item icons sparingly, and only when one clearly represents the item.** Icons speed finding and clarify effects, so reserve them for the most common actions and key features, file system locations, connected devices, visual concepts like rotating an image, and user content like folders. (`menus`)
- **Give every item in a group an icon, or none.** A uniform treatment keeps the group visually consistent and balanced. (`menus`)
- **List important or frequent items first.** People start scanning from the top, so high-priority items there are found without reading the whole menu. (`menus`)
- **Group logically related items with separators, even when their importance differs.** Groups like Copy, Cut, and Paste help people remember where commands live, so the rarer Paste and Match Style belongs with them. A separator appears as a horizontal line or a short gap. (`menus`)
- **Keep menus short, except menus of user-defined or dynamic content.** Long menus take time and attention, so split them or use a submenu, like difficulty levels under New Game. Safari's History and Bookmarks may grow and scroll because people expect them to hold everything they add. (`menus`)
- **Use submenus sparingly, and consider one when a term repeats in more than two items of a group.** Each submenu adds complexity and hides its items. Sort by Date, Score, and Time become one Sort by item whose submenu lists Date, Score, and Time. (`menus`)
- **Keep submenus to one level and about five items, and never indent items instead.** Several levels are hard to reveal, a longer submenu may deserve its own menu, and indentation is inconsistent with the system and doesn't express how items relate. (`menus`)
- **Keep a submenu available even when all its nested items are unavailable.** Like any menu item, it must let people open it and learn what commands it contains. A chevron after its label marks it as a submenu. (`menus`)
- **For an on/off attribute, consider one toggled item whose label states the current state, adding a verb if the label is ambiguous.** Show Map becomes Hide Map, and Turn HDR On is clearer than HDR On, which could be an action or a state. (`menus`)
- **Show both items instead of one toggled item when people need to see both states.** A game can list Take Account Online and Take Account Offline, with only Take Account Offline available while the account is online. (`menus`)
- **Mark attributes in effect with checkmarks, and consider an item that clears several at once.** Checkmarks are easy to scan, as in Format > Font, and an item like Plain removes all applied formatting in one step. (`menus`)
- **In games, let players navigate menus with the platform's default interaction, like touch in iOS and iPadOS.** People expect the same interactions they use for other menus on the device. (`menus`)
- **Keep in-game menus easy to open and read on every platform you support.** Each platform has its own best font and target sizes, and scaling game content to a phone screen can shrink menus, so enlarge tap targets or present the content another way. (`menus`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Pick one of three menu layouts: small, medium, or large.** Small puts a row of four symbol-only items above the list, medium a row of three with a symbol above a short label, and large, the default, lists every item. (`menus`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Use the small or medium layout only when it streamlines choices.** Medium suits three important, frequent actions, like Scan, Lock, and Pin in Notes. Small suits closely related actions that appear as a group, like Bold, Italic, Underline, and Strikethrough, each with a recognizable symbol. (`menus`)

## Context menus (`context-menus`)

- **Remember that a context menu is hidden by default, so people might not know it's there.** They reveal it with touch and hold in iOS and iPadOS, or a Control-click or Magic Trackpad secondary click in macOS and iPadOS. On a Mac it's also called a contextual menu. (`context-menus`)
- **Include only the commands people most likely need in the current context, and keep the list short.** A context menu isn't for advanced or rarely used items: Mail's message menu offers reply and move, not editing or mailbox management, and long menus are hard to scan and scroll. (`context-menus`)
- **Support context menus consistently throughout the app.** If some items have one and others don't, people won't know where to use the feature and may think there's a problem. (`context-menus`)
- **Hide unavailable items instead of dimming them.** Unlike a regular menu, a context menu shows only actions relevant to the selected view or content. In macOS, Cut, Copy, and Paste may still appear unavailable. (`context-menus`)
- **Put the most frequent items where people look first.** People read from the part nearest the finger or pointer that opened the menu, and it can open above or below the content, so reverse the order to match. (`context-menus`)
- **Keep submenus to one level, with titles that predict their contents.** A submenu can shorten the menu and clarify commands, but deeper levels complicate the experience and are hard to navigate. (`context-menus`)
- **Group items with separators, in no more than about three groups.** As in other menus, groups help people scan the menu more quickly. (`context-menus`)
- **Show keyboard shortcuts in the main menus, not in context menus.** A context menu is already a shortcut to task-specific commands, so key equivalents there are redundant. (`context-menus`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Put destructive items like Delete or Remove at the end of the menu, marked as destructive.** The system can show them in red, warning people that they can destroy data. (`context-menus`)
- **Skip a title unless it clarifies the menu's effect, but give every item a short, clear label.** Context menus seldom show titles: marking several Mail messages shows one with the message count, a reminder that the command affects all of them. (`context-menus`)
- **Represent actions with the system's icons, like Copy, Share, and Delete, wherever they appear.** Familiar icons help people recognize common actions throughout the app. (`context-menus`)
- **(iPadOS) Consider a context menu for creating a new object.** People reveal it with a long press or a secondary click with a trackpad or keyboard, as Files creates a new folder from the space between existing files and folders. (`context-menus`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Show a graphical preview that clarifies the menu's target.** Notes and Mail show a condensed version of the item so people confirm it's the one they intend, and people can sometimes tap the preview to open it or drag it elsewhere. (`context-menus`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Match the preview's clipping path to the preview image's shape.** The preview animates out of the content as the screen behind it dims, and a matching clip keeps contours like rounded corners from changing. (`context-menus`)

## Pop-up and pull-down buttons (`pop-up-buttons`, `pull-down-buttons`)

- **Give a pop-up button a useful default, ideally the item most people want.** It shows that default until people choose, and after a choice the menu closes and the button can show the current selection. (`pop-up-buttons`)
- **Let people predict a pop-up button's options without opening it.** An introductory label, or a button label that describes the button's effect, gives the options context. (`pop-up-buttons`)
- **Consider a pop-up button when space is limited and people don't need every option visible all the time.** It's a space-efficient way to present a wide array of choices. (`pop-up-buttons`)
- **Add a Custom item, if needed, for options people need only in some situations.** It avoids cluttering the interface with rarely needed items or controls, and explanatory text below the list can say how the options work. (`pop-up-buttons`)
- **(iPadOS) In a popover or modal view, consider a pop-up button instead of a disclosure indicator for a list item's options.** People choose without navigating to a detail view, which suits a small, well-defined set of options. (`pop-up-buttons`)
- **Never hide all of a view's actions in one pull-down button.** Primary actions must be easy to discover, not buried in a menu people must open before they can do anything. (`pull-down-buttons`)
- **Mark destructive pull-down items and confirm them before they run.** They appear in red, and choosing one opens an action sheet in iOS or a popover in iPadOS, which needs deliberate dismissal, so people are less likely to lose data. (`pull-down-buttons`)
- **Show a pull-down menu title only if it adds meaning.** The button's content plus descriptive items usually give people all the context they need. (`pull-down-buttons`)
- **Add an icon after a pull-down item's label only when it clarifies the item.** SF Symbols keep the look familiar and stay aligned with the text at every scale. (`pull-down-buttons`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Consider a More pull-down button for items that don't need prominent positions, weighing its compactness against discoverability.** People know it offers more for the current context, but its ellipsis icon doesn't help them predict what's inside. (`pull-down-buttons`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Consider revealing a pull-down menu with a gesture on a button, too.** Safari shows tab actions like New Tab and Close All Tabs when people touch and hold its Tabs button. (`pull-down-buttons`)

## The menu bar (`the-menu-bar`)

- **(iPadOS, macOS) Support the standard menus in their standard order: the app menu, File, Edit, Format, View, app-specific menus, Window, Help.** People rely on the menu bar to learn what an app does, and the system implements many standard items for you, like Edit > Copy for selected text. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(macOS) Expect the Apple menu at the leading end and menu bar extras at the trailing end.** The Apple menu always comes first and can't be changed or removed. When space runs short, the system favors menus and essential extras, tightening and truncating titles. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS) Design for a menu bar that stays hidden until people reveal it.** They move the pointer to the top edge or swipe down, and it appears centered in the status bar's space, with no Apple menu, menu bar extras, About, Services, or app-visibility items, and with window controls when the app is full screen. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Always show the same items, disabling unavailable ones instead of hiding them.** Visible items teach people what the app supports, even when an action isn't available in the current context. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Support the standard keyboard shortcuts of the standard items you include, and add custom ones only when necessary.** People expect the shortcuts they know for Copy, Cut, Paste, Save, and Print. iPadOS shortcuts follow the macOS patterns. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Use the system's icons for actions like Copy, Share, and Delete.** The same icons everywhere help people recognize common actions throughout the app. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Prefer short, one-word menu titles.** Display size and menu bar extras affect spacing, and one-word titles take little room and scan easily. Use title-style capitalization when a title needs more words. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(macOS) Put About first in the app menu, alone in its group, with a short app name and no version number.** Prefer a name of 16 characters or fewer, and reuse it in Hide and Quit. The menu bar shows the app name in bold so people can identify the active app. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Use Settings… only for app-level settings, and list custom configuration items right after it in the same group.** Document-specific settings belong in the File menu. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS) Reserve the YourAppName > Settings item for opening the app's page in iPadOS Settings.** Link to the app's own internal preferences with a separate item beneath it in the same group, alongside any other custom app-wide options. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) In the File menu, name the type of item New creates, and add an ellipsis to Open when people choose the item in a separate interface.** Calendar uses New Event and New Calendar. An app that handles no files can rename or remove the File menu. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) List Open Recent items by document name, never by path, most recently opened first.** People recognize names, not paths, and the submenu typically includes a Clear Menu item. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Save documents automatically as people work, and prefer Duplicate to Save As, Export, Copy To, or Save To.** Autosave spares people from choosing File > Save, and those other items don't clarify how the new file relates to the original. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) For a new document, prompt for a name and location on save.** If people can save a file in several formats, prefer a pop-up menu in the Save sheet for choosing the format. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Reserve Export As for formats the app doesn't typically handle, and include Page Setup only for document-specific printing parameters.** Global or frequently changed settings, like the printer's name or the number of copies, belong in the Print panel. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) In a tab-based window, Close Tab replaces Close, so consider adding Close Window to close the whole window at once.** Option turns Close into Close All and Close Tab into Close Other Tabs. Consider Close File if the app opens several views of one file. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Name the target in Undo and Redo, and call deletion Delete, not Erase or Clear.** Undo Typing or Undo Paste and Match Style says what will be reversed, and choosing Delete must match pressing the Delete key. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Keep an Edit menu even in an app that isn't document-based, but consider moving Find items to the File menu.** Editing and Clipboard commands apply to any editable content, while Find fits File better when people search for files or other objects. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Include a Format menu, with Font and Text submenus, only if the app supports formatted text editing.** It adjusts text formatting in the current document or text container, so an app without formatted text can leave it out. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Provide a View menu even if the app supports only some view functions, with show and hide titles that match the current state.** A full-screen-only app still offers Enter Full Screen, and a visible toolbar gets Hide Toolbar. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Keep appearance in the View menu and window management in the Window menu.** View shows and hides the tab bar, toolbar, and sidebar and enters full screen, Window navigates and arranges windows, and closing a window belongs to File > Close. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS) With tab-style navigation, consider listing each tab in the View menu, with a key binding for each.** Each tab is a different view of the app, so the View menu is a natural extra way to move between tabs. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) List every custom command in app-specific menus between View and Window, even when it's available elsewhere.** People look there for commands, especially on first use, and menu bar items get shortcuts and Full Keyboard Access, like Safari's History and Bookmarks. Leaving out even advanced commands makes them hard for everyone to find. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Order app-specific menus to mirror the app's hierarchy, from most to least general or commonly used.** Mail lists Mailbox, Message, and Format because mailboxes contain messages and messages contain formatting. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Provide a Window menu even for a single window, with Minimize and Zoom.** People using Full Keyboard Access can then use the keyboard for both. Avoid using Zoom for full screen, and list Enter Full Screen here only if there's no View menu. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Consider Window menu items that show and hide panels, except the font and text color panels.** Panels appear only when people need them, and the Format menu already lists those two. List open windows alphabetically, never panels or other modal views. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Keep the Help menu short, with a separator between the main help and extras like release notes.** Too many choices overwhelm people who need help, so consider linking extras from the help documentation. In macOS, the Help Book format adds a search field automatically. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Use dynamic menu items rarely, with one modifier key, and never as the only way to do something.** They're hidden by default, as Option turns Minimize into Minimize All, and pressing several keys while choosing an item is awkward. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS, macOS) Put dynamic menu items primarily in menu bar menus.** In contextual or Dock menus they become even harder for people to discover. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS) Make every function reachable in the app's own interface, never only in the menu bar.** The menu bar is often hidden when the app runs full screen, and dynamic items need a hardware keyboard, so don't use it as a catch-all. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(iPadOS) Consider grouping items into submenus more often than on Mac.** iPad menu rows are taller so they're easy to tap, and some iPads have small screens, so submenus conserve vertical space. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(macOS) Represent a menu bar extra with a symbol or interface icon drawn in black and clear.** The system recolors the black areas so it looks right on dark and light menu bars and when selected. The menu bar is 24 pt tall. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(macOS) Open a menu, not a popover, when people click your menu bar extra.** Reserve a popover for functionality that's too complex for a menu. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(macOS) Let people decide whether your menu bar extra appears, usually through a setting in the app's settings window.** Consider offering the choice during setup so people discover it. (`the-menu-bar`)
- **(macOS) Never rely on your menu bar extra being visible, and expose its functionality elsewhere, like a Dock menu.** The system hides extras regularly and you can't predict their location, but a Dock menu is always available while the app runs. (`the-menu-bar`)

## Dock menus and Home Screen quick actions (`dock-menus`, `home-screen-quick-actions`)

- **(macOS) Make every custom Dock menu item available elsewhere too.** Not everyone uses Dock menus, so offer the same commands in the menu bar or the interface. (`dock-menus`)
- **(macOS) Fill a Dock menu with high-value items: open or recent windows and the few actions useful when the app isn't frontmost or has no open windows.** Mail adds getting new mail and composing a message to its list of open windows. Label and group items as in any menu. (`dock-menus`)
- **(macOS) Expect the system to add its own Dock menu items, which vary with whether the app is open.** People reveal the menu with a secondary click on the app's Dock icon, and Safari's offers viewing a current window or creating a new one. (`dock-menus`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Offer up to four Home Screen quick actions, the iPhone and iPad counterpart of a Dock menu, for compelling, high-value tasks.** People expect at least one, and Maps lets people search nearby or get directions home without opening the app first. (`home-screen-quick-actions`, `dock-menus`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Build each quick action from a title, an optional subtitle, and an interface icon.** People touch and hold an app icon to see them beside system items for removing the app and editing the Home Screen. The icon sits left or right by the app's position, and text stays left-aligned in left-to-right languages. (`home-screen-quick-actions`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Give each quick action a succinct title that says what happens, without the app name.** Directions Home and New Message are clear, a subtitle adds context only when needed, like Mail's unread counts, and short, localizable text avoids truncation. (`home-screen-quick-actions`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Use a familiar SF Symbol or interface icon for each quick action, never an emoji.** Emojis are full color, while quick action symbols are monochromatic and adapt to Dark Mode to keep their contrast. Draw a custom icon with the Quick Action Icon Template. (`home-screen-quick-actions`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Change dynamic quick actions only in ways people can predict.** Updating them by location, recent activity, time of day, or settings keeps them relevant, as Messages lists recent conversations. (`home-screen-quick-actions`)
