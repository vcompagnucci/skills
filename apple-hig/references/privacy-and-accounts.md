# Privacy and accounts

How an app asks for data and permissions, and handles accounts and sign-in, on iPhone, iPad, and Mac. Apple's position: ask only for the data and sign-in a feature needs, when it needs them, through system flows and in plain words, and make leaving as easy as joining. (`privacy`, `managing-accounts`, `sign-in-with-apple`)

**Contents:** Choosing sign-in · Privacy · Managing accounts · Sign in with Apple · ID Verifier · Key source pages

## Choosing sign-in

- **Require an account only if core functionality needs it, and ask for sign-in as late as possible.** People often abandon apps that force sign-in first: a shopping app can let people browse and ask at purchase. Briefly say why, and what people gain. (`managing-accounts`, `sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Offer Sign in with Apple first, then passkeys, and keep passwords only with two-factor authentication.** Sign in with Apple is a sign-in people trust without another account to remember, and a passkey needs only a user name. Never invent an authentication scheme. (`managing-accounts`, `privacy`)

## Privacy (`privacy`)

- **Provide details about your privacy practices and the privacy-relevant data you collect when you submit a new or updated app.** The App Store shows them on your product page so people can make an informed decision before downloading, and you manage them in App Store Connect. (`privacy`)
- **Request access only to data a feature actually needs, and make each permission request as specific as possible.** Asking for more, or asking before people show interest in the feature, makes your app hard to trust, while specific requests give people precise control over their data. (`privacy`)
- **Be transparent about how your app collects and uses people's data.** People are less comfortable sharing data when they don't understand exactly how you'll use it. Always respect their choice to use Hide My Email or Mail Privacy Protection, and understand your obligations around app tracking. (`privacy`)
- **Process data on the device where possible.** In iOS, for example, the Apple Neural Engine and custom CreateML models can process data right on the device, avoiding lengthy and potentially risky round trips to a remote server. (`privacy`)
- **Adopt system-defined privacy protections and follow security best practices.** Rely on system-provided security technologies to store information locally, authorize people for specific operations, and transport information across a network. In iOS 15 and later, CloudKit can encrypt and manage keys for more data types, like strings, numbers, and dates. (`privacy`)
- **You must request permission to access personal data, user-generated content, protected resources, device capabilities, and the advertising identifier, which supports app tracking.** Examples include location, health, and financial data, emails, messages, calendar data, photos, Bluetooth peripherals, Wi-Fi connections, local networks, the camera, and the microphone. (`privacy`)
- **Supply copy explaining why your app needs access, and let the system's standard alert present each request.** People can view your description again, and change their choice, in Settings > Privacy. (`privacy`)
- **Request permission only when your app clearly needs access, ideally when people use a feature that requires it.** People distrust a request with no obvious need. A location button, for example, lets people share their location once they show interest in a feature that needs it. (`privacy`)
- **Avoid requesting permission at launch unless your app can't function without the data or resource.** People are less bothered by a launch-time request when the reason is obvious, as when a navigation app needs their location before they can benefit from it. (`privacy`)
- **Write a purpose string, or usage description string, that clearly says how your app uses what you request.** The alert shows it after your app name and before the buttons that grant or deny permission. Aim for one brief, complete sentence that's straightforward and specific. (`privacy`)
- **Write purpose strings in sentence case and active voice, with a period at the end.** "The app records during the night to detect snoring sounds." says how and why. "Microphone access is needed for a better experience." is passive and vague, and "Turn on microphone access." gives no justification. (`privacy`)
- **Expect the system alert's buttons to vary with the request.** A location alert offers Allow Once, Allow While Using App, and Don't Allow. A photos alert offers Select Photos, Allow Access to All Photos, and Don't Allow. A contacts alert puts Don't Allow and Allow side by side. (`privacy`)
- **Let the current context explain a permission request, and show a custom screen or window before the alert only when more detail is essential.** Pre-alert rules cover requests for protected data and resources, including the camera, microphone, location, contacts, calendar, and tracking. (`privacy`)
- **Give a pre-alert screen only one button, like Continue or Next, that clearly opens the system alert.** People feel manipulated by a second button that diverts them from their choice, or by an Allow button resembling the alert's in meaning and weight, which leads them to allow without meaning to. (`privacy`)
- **Don't include other actions in a pre-alert screen unless you need them to obtain legal consent.** In particular, don't let people leave the screen or window without viewing the system alert, as a Close or Cancel option would. (`privacy`)
- **If you perform app tracking as soon as people launch your app, show the system alert before you collect any tracking data.** App tracking is a sensitive issue, though in some cases a custom screen or window describing the benefits of tracking can make sense. (`privacy`)
- **Never put a custom screen before the system permission alert that could confuse or mislead.** Many people dismiss alerts quickly without reading, and a screen built to exploit that and steer their answer gets the app rejected in App Store review. (`privacy`)
- **On a pre-tracking screen, never offer incentives, mimic a request, show an image of the alert, or annotate the screen behind it.** These designs, like a $100 credit for allowing tracking, cause rejection. A consent screen before or after the App Tracking Transparency alert, to comply with local privacy laws, is allowed. (`privacy`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Use the location button so people can grant temporary location access at the moment a task needs it.** Core Location provides it, and while its appearance can vary to match your UI, it always communicates location sharing in a way people instantly recognize. (`privacy`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Know that a location button's first tap shows a standard alert, and each later tap grants one-time access.** The alert explains how the button limits access and reminds people of the location indicator. People confirm once, and each one-time authorization expires when they stop using your app. (`privacy`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) A location button tap equals choosing Allow Once only when your app has no authorization status.** If people previously chose While Using the App, tapping it doesn't change your app's status. (`privacy`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Consider the location button for one-off location sharing inside a feature.** It fits attaching a location to a message or post, finding a store, or identifying a building, plant, or animal, and spares people who often pick Allow Once from repeated alerts. (`privacy`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Consider customizing the location button to harmonize with your UI.** You can choose a system-provided title like Current Location or Share My Current Location, a filled or outlined glyph, a background color, a title and glyph color, and the corner radius. (`privacy`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Leave the location button's other visual attributes alone, and make sure its text fits.** Fixed attributes help people recognize and trust the button. The system warns about low contrast or too much translucency, and the text must fit untruncated at every accessibility text size and in translation. (`privacy`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Fix consistent problems in a customized location button, or it stops granting location.** The system then won't give your app the device location when people tap it, and a location button that doesn't work as people expect can cost your app their trust. (`privacy`)
- **Avoid relying solely on passwords for authentication.** Replace them with passkeys where possible, and require two-factor authentication if you keep passwords. To further protect apps people stay logged in to, use biometric identification like Face ID or Touch ID. (`privacy`)
- **Store sensitive information in a keychain, and never store passwords or other secure content in plain-text files.** A keychain gives people a secure, predictable experience with their private information, and an encrypted keychain is much safer than a file, even one with restricted permissions. (`privacy`)
- **Don't invent your own authentication scheme.** When the app needs authentication, use what the system provides: passkeys, Sign in with Apple, or Password AutoFill. (`privacy`)
- **(macOS) Sign your app with a valid Developer ID if you distribute it outside the App Store.** Developer ID identifies you as an Apple developer and confirms that your app is safe to use. (`privacy`)
- **(macOS) Protect people's data with app sandboxing.** Sandboxing gives your app access to system resources and user data while protecting it from malware, and all apps submitted to the Mac App Store require it. (`privacy`)
- **(macOS) Avoid making assumptions about who is signed in.** Because of fast user switching, several people may be active on the same Mac at once. (`privacy`)

## Managing accounts (`managing-accounts`)

- **Require an account only when core functionality depends on it.** Otherwise people should be able to use the app or game without one. When an account is required, consider Sign in with Apple: one trusted sign-in, and no extra credentials to remember. (`managing-accounts`)
- **Ask people to sign in only in exchange for value, and explain the benefits of an account and how to sign up.** In your sign-in view, give a brief, friendly description of why you need an account and its benefits, like personalization, more features, or synced data. (`managing-accounts`, `sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Delay sign-in for as long as possible.** People often abandon apps that force sign-in before anything useful, so let them get a sense of the app first, as a shopping app lets people browse until they're ready to buy and a live-streaming app lets them explore content before streaming. (`managing-accounts`, `sign-in-with-apple`)
- **If you don't use Sign in with Apple, prefer a passkey.** Passkeys simplify account creation and authentication without passwords: people simply provide their user name to create an account or sign in. If you must keep passwords, require two-factor authentication. (`managing-accounts`)
- **Always identify the authentication method you offer, and refer only to methods available in the current context.** Title a Face ID button "Sign In with Face ID", not "Sign In", and check the device's capabilities so you never mention Face ID on a device that doesn't offer it. (`managing-accounts`)
- **Usually skip an in-app switch for turning on biometric authentication.** People already enable biometrics at the system level, so a second switch in the app is redundant and can confuse them. (`managing-accounts`)
- **Don't call account authentication a "passcode".** People use a passcode to unlock the device and for Apple services, so the word suggests you want that same code in your app or game. (`managing-accounts`)
- **If you help people create an account in your app or game, also help them delete it, not just deactivate it.** This holds even when they bought their subscription elsewhere, and if they created the account with Sign in with Apple, revoke its associated tokens when they delete it. (`managing-accounts`)
- **Comply with your region's legal requirements for account deletion and the right to be forgotten.** If the law compels you to keep certain accounts or information, like digital health records, or to follow a specific deletion process, clearly describe the situation so people understand it. (`managing-accounts`)
- **Provide a clear way to initiate account deletion within your app or game.** If people can't delete their account in the app, you must link directly to the webpage where they can, and make the link easy to discover, not buried in your Privacy Policy or Terms of Service. (`managing-accounts`)
- **Provide a consistent account-deletion experience in your app or game and on your website.** Avoid making one version of the deletion flow longer or more complicated than the other. (`managing-accounts`)
- **Consider letting people schedule account deletion for a future date, and offer immediate deletion too.** People can appreciate using their remaining services, or waiting until their subscription auto-renews, before deleting their account. (`managing-accounts`)
- **Tell people when account deletion will complete, and notify them when it's finished.** Fully deleting an account can take a while, and keeping people informed about its status lets them know what to expect. (`managing-accounts`)
- **With in-app purchases, explain what happens to billing when people delete their account.** An auto-renewable subscription keeps billing through Apple until it's canceled, even after the account is gone, so tell people how to cancel, ask for a refund, and manage purchases. (`managing-accounts`)
- **If your TV provider app requires people to sign in, use TV Provider Authentication.** Many TV providers let people sign in at the system level instead of app by app, which gives the most efficient onboarding experience. (`managing-accounts`)
- **Avoid displaying a sign-out option when people are signed in to their TV provider at the system level.** If your app must include one, invoking it needs to prompt people to go to Settings > TV Provider to sign out of their account. (`managing-accounts`)
- **Never tell people to sign out through privacy controls.** The TV provider settings under Settings > Privacy only manage which apps can use a TV provider account, and they don't sign anyone out. (`managing-accounts`)

## Sign in with Apple (`sign-in-with-apple`)

- **Sign in with Apple lets people sign in or sign up with the Apple Account they already have.** They skip forms, email verification, and passwords, and if you ask for a name and email, they can share a unique, random address that relays messages to their personal email. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **You can offer Sign in with Apple in every version of your app or website, on all platforms, including non-Apple ones.** It lets people authenticate with Face ID or Touch ID, has two-factor authentication built in, and Apple doesn't use it to profile people or their activity in apps. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **If you require an account, ask people to set it up before you offer any sign-in options.** Start by explaining why you require an account. Once people complete setup, let them choose a convenient way to sign in, offering Sign in with Apple and any other methods you support. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Consider letting people link an existing account to Sign in with Apple.** They get its convenience while keeping their account's information. You can suggest linking when the email people share matches an existing account, or in the settings view of people who signed in with a user name and password. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **In a commerce app, wait until after people make a purchase to ask them to create an account.** With guest checkout, offer a quick way to create one once the transaction completes, like on the Apple Pay order confirmation page, without asking again for a name and email people already provided. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **As soon as Sign in with Apple completes, welcome people to their new account.** Help them use it right away, and don't delay the experience by asking for information you don't require. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Indicate when people are currently signed in.** A phrase like "Using Sign in with Apple" in a settings or account interface helps people confirm their sign-in method. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Minimize your data requests during account setup, and explain why you need any additional data.** People appreciate Sign in with Apple for its privacy and convenience, so build on that trust when you need data like a birth date or region of residence, and clearly display the data you receive. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Clarify whether the additional data you request is required or just recommended.** For legally or contractually required data, like agreeing to terms of service, region of residence, birth date, or real-identity information, make clear people must supply it to finish setup. For optional data, say so and explain its benefits. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Don't ask people to supply a password.** Not having to create and memorize more passwords is a key benefit of Sign in with Apple, so ask for one only if people have stopped using Sign in with Apple with your app or website. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Avoid asking for a personal email address when people share a private relay address.** Respect that choice. If customer service or retail needs an email, show people their relay address, send them to Settings > Apple Account > Password & Security > Apps using Apple Account, or use an order or phone number. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Give people a chance to engage with your app before asking for optional data.** As they use it, point out where sharing more helps, like a phone number for real-time text updates or social network information to play games with friends. If they decline, don't block their account or any feature. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Be transparent about the data you collect, for example by welcoming people with the name or email they shared.** That shows how you use the information and, for a relay address, where to find it later. Data you collect but never display makes people wonder why you asked. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Prominently display a Sign in with Apple button.** Make it no smaller than your other sign-in buttons, and don't make people scroll to see it. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Prefer the system-provided Sign in with Apple buttons.** They guarantee an Apple-approved appearance, keep ideal proportions as you change the style, translate the title automatically, let you match the corner radius to your UI in iOS and macOS, and give VoiceOver an alternative text label. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Choose the title variant that fits your sign-in terminology, and use it consistently throughout your interface.** In iOS, macOS, and the web, the system offers Sign in with Apple, Sign up with Apple, and Continue with Apple. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Choose the white, white with outline, or black style by the background behind the button.** Use white on dark backgrounds that provide sufficient contrast, never on light ones. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **On white or light backgrounds, use black, or white with outline when the background doesn't contrast enough with a white fill.** Never use black on black or dark backgrounds, and on dark or saturated ones use white instead of the outline, which adds visual clutter there. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Adjust the corner radius to match the other buttons in your app.** The button has rounded corners by default, and in iOS, macOS, and the web you can square the corners or make it a capsule. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Maintain the system button's minimum size of 140 by 30 pt in iOS, macOS, and the web, and a margin of at least 1/10 of its height.** That's 280 by 60 px at @2x. Be mindful that the title's length varies by locale. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Create a custom Sign in with Apple button only if your interface requires it.** In iOS, macOS, or the web, a custom button can align logos across several sign-in buttons, show only a logo, or adjust the font, bezel, or background to coordinate with your UI. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Make sure people can instantly identify a custom button as Sign in with Apple.** If it differs too much from the standard button, people may not feel comfortable using it to set up an account or sign in, and App Review evaluates every custom Sign in with Apple button. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Use only the Apple logo artwork from Apple Design Resources, and never create a custom Apple logo.** The artwork comes in PNG, SVG, and PDF, in black and white versions, for logo-only buttons and for buttons with a logo and text. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Rely on the padding built into the downloadable logo files.** Logo-only files carry horizontal and vertical padding that keeps the logo proportioned to the button, and files for buttons with text add horizontal padding for a minimum margin from the leading edge and the title. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Use the logo file to position the Apple logo in a button, and never use the Apple logo as a button.** Match the logo file's height to the button's height, and don't crop the file or add vertical padding. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Keep a custom button's title, shape, and colors consistent with the system button.** Use only Sign in with Apple, Sign up with Apple, or Continue with Apple. Logo-and-text buttons are always rectangular, logo-only ones circular or rectangular, and the logo and title are both black or both white, never custom colors. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **You can change a custom button's title font and case, background, corner radius, bezel, and shadow to fit your design.** Adjust the font's weight and size, capitalize every letter, add a subtle texture or gradient to a background that stays black or white, or add a stroke or drop shadow. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **For a logo-and-text button, choose the logo file format by the button's height.** Vector SVG and PDF files work at any height, and PNG only in 44 pt tall buttons, the default and recommended height in iOS. Small, medium, and large logos let you match logo sizes across sign-up buttons. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Prefer the system font for the title, and keep the system's proportions whatever font you use.** With the system font, the title's size is 43% of the button's height, so the height is 233% of the font size, rounded: 19 pt in a 44 pt button, 24 pt in a 56 pt one. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **In general, preserve the title's capitalization, with capitals only on the first word, Sign or Continue, and on Apple.** All other letters stay lowercase, so avoid changing this unless your interface uses only uppercase. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Keep the title and logo vertically aligned within the button.** Align the title to the button's vertical middle, then add the logo image at the button's full height. Because the image includes top and bottom padding, the title, logo, and button stay aligned. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Inset the logo from the leading edge if necessary, and keep at least 8% of the button's width between the title and the right edge.** Adjusting the logo's inset lets you align the Apple logo horizontally with other authentication logos. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Maintain a logo-and-text custom button's minimum size of 140 by 30 pt, and a margin around it of at least 1/10 of its height.** That's 280 by 60 px at @2x. Be mindful that the title's length varies by locale. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **For a logo-only button, choose the logo file format by the button's size.** Use the vector SVG and PDF files for buttons of any size, and PNG only in buttons that measure 44x44 pt. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Don't pad a logo-only image horizontally.** The logo-only Sign in with Apple button is always square (1:1), and its artwork already carries the right padding on every side. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Use a mask to change the logo-only image's default square shape, like a circle or rounded rectangle for all your logo-only sign-in buttons.** Never crop the Apple-provided artwork to reduce its built-in padding, never use the logo by itself, and avoid adding padding. (`sign-in-with-apple`)
- **Maintain a margin of at least 1/10 of a logo-only button's height around it.** That's the same minimum margin as the system button and custom buttons with text. (`sign-in-with-apple`)

## ID Verifier (`id-verifier`)

- **(iOS) Use ID Verifier to read mobile IDs in person on iPhone, without external hardware.** Starting in iOS 17, iPhone reads ISO18013-5 compliant mobile IDs, so concert venue staff can verify ages, and customers present only the minimum data, without handing over an ID card or showing their device. (`id-verifier`)
- **(iOS) Make a Data Transfer request only when a legal verification requirement means you must store or process data.** Otherwise use Display Only, which shows data like a name or age beside the person's portrait in system UI, never sending it to your app. Data Transfer needs an extra entitlement. (`id-verifier`)
- **(iOS) Ask only for the data the current verification needs.** People may lose trust when you ask for more, so to confirm a customer is at least a minimum age, request an age threshold, not their current age or birth date. (`id-verifier`)
- **(iOS) If your app qualifies for Apple Business Register, register for ID Verifier there.** The system then displays your official organization name and logo on customers' devices in the verification UI, so people see essential information about your organization when you make a request. (`id-verifier`)
- **(iOS) Start verification from a button labeled like Verify Age or Verify Identity, never with the Apple logo, and avoid NFC or QR code symbols.** Verify Age fits a simple age check, like at a concert hall, and Verify Identity a detailed match, like a name and birth date at a rental car pickup. (`id-verifier`)
- **(iOS) In a Display Only request, help the person using your app give feedback on the visual confirmation they perform.** When the reader shows the customer's portrait, buttons like Matches Person and Doesn't Match Person let your app receive an approved or rejected value in the response. (`id-verifier`)
