# Icons and symbols

How an app draws its interface icons and its app icon. For Apple, an interface icon is a simple, familiar metaphor that matches adjacent text, ideally an SF Symbol whose rendering, variant, and animation each carry meaning. The app icon expresses one concept in layers that the system masks, lights, and adapts to every appearance. (`icons`, `sf-symbols`, `app-icons`)

## Choosing symbols, custom icons, and variants

- **Use an SF Symbol wherever an interface icon can appear, like toolbars, tab bars, context menus, and inline text.** Thousands of configurable symbols align automatically with the San Francisco font in every weight and size. Symbols and features introduced in a given year aren't available on earlier systems. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Keep an interface icon, or glyph, to streamlined shapes and touches of color, and save rich detail for the app icon.** An interface icon communicates one straightforward idea, while an app icon can use shading, texture, and highlights to evoke personality. Both icons and symbols draw shapes in black and clear, so the system can recolor them. (`icons`)
- **Use the standard symbol for a common action in menus, toolbars, and buttons.** People then recognize Share (square with up arrow), Delete (trash can), More (ellipsis), Done (checkmark), and Close (X) everywhere. The full mapping is under Interface icons. (`icons`)
- **Make a custom interface icon a vector (PDF or SVG), or a custom SF Symbol.** The system scales vectors for high-resolution displays, while PNG doesn't scale and needs multiple versions. A custom symbol can take a scale that matches adjacent text. (`icons`)
- **Never use SF Symbols, or look-alikes, in app icons, logos, or other trademarked uses.** The terms prohibit it. Never replicate or customize the symbols for Apple products, which are copyrighted. The SF Symbols app marks noncustomizable symbols with an Info icon. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Variable color to show change, the hierarchical rendering mode to show depth.** Variable color fills layers as a value crosses thresholds from 0 to 100%, like the three-wave speaker showing three sound ranges plus silence. Unchanging layers, like the speaker, opt out. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Pick the design variant by state and context.** A slash shows something is unavailable and fill shows selection. Outline suits toolbars, lists, and symbols beside text, fill adds emphasis in iOS tab bars and swipe actions, and enclosures help at small sizes. (`sf-symbols`)

## SF Symbols (`sf-symbols`)

- **Choose among four rendering modes, which color a symbol's layers differently.** Monochrome applies one color to every layer, hierarchical varies one color's opacity by layer level, palette uses one color per layer, and multicolor applies intrinsic colors, like a green leaf or a red trash.slash for data loss. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Check that the rendering mode stays legible in every context, even when automatic.** Size and contrast change how well details read, so another mode may read better than the automatic pick in some places. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Color symbols with system-provided colors.** In any rendering mode they adapt automatically to accessibility accommodations, vibrancy, and Dark Mode. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Use gradients, in SF Symbols 7 and later, mainly at larger sizes.** A gradient derives smoothly from one source color and works in every rendering mode, with system or custom colors and on custom symbols, and though it renders at any size, it looks best large. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Match a symbol's weight to adjacent text, and change emphasis with scale, not point size.** Each of the nine weights matches a San Francisco weight, and the three scales (small, medium, large) follow the font's cap height, so weights stay matched. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Let the view pick outline or fill when it can, and combine slash or enclosed variants with either.** Outline, the most common variant, has no solid areas and resembles text, and most symbols also come in fill. An iOS tab bar prefers fill, while a toolbar takes outline. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Rely on the language- and script-specific symbol variants.** They adapt automatically when the device language changes, covering Latin, Arabic, Hebrew, Hindi, Thai, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Cyrillic, Devanagari, and several Indic numeral systems. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Apply symbol animations judiciously.** A view has no limit on animations, but too many overwhelm the interface and distract people. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Make each animation serve a clear purpose in communicating the symbol's intent.** Each movement implies a kind of action or response, so consider how people might read an animation, or a combination, and whether it confuses them. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Use symbol animations to communicate information efficiently, in your app's tone.** They give visual feedback that something happened and present complex information simply in little space, and what they convey should fit the brand and the app's style. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Pick a feedback animation by what happened.** Bounce: an action occurred or is needed. Scale: selection or feedback that persists until it changes. Replace: a state change, with Magic Replace between related symbols. Wiggle: a call to action people might overlook. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Pick an activity animation by the kind of work.** Pulse and breathe: ongoing activity. Variable color: progress or activity like connecting. Rotate: a task in progress. Draw On and Draw Off: progress like a download, or direction. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Choose a Replace configuration by the kind of state change.** Down-up signals a change in state, up-up adds forward progression, and off-up emphasizes the next available state or action. Magic Replace, the default, transitions related shapes and falls back to down-up between unrelated ones. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Choose cumulative or iterative variable color animation.** Cumulative keeps each layer's color until the cycle completes, iterative colors one layer at a time, and closed-loop symbols, like a circular progress indicator, loop without a visible break. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Set how an animation plays: once from start to finish, or repeating until a condition is met.** You can also change its speed or reverse it before repeating. Animations work on every symbol, rendering mode, weight, and scale, and Appear and Disappear bring a symbol gradually in or out of view. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Build a custom symbol from the exported template of a similar symbol.** Match the system set's level of detail, optical weight, alignment, position, and perspective, and keep it simple, recognizable, inclusive, and directly related to its action or content. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Annotate each layer of a custom symbol with a color or a hierarchy level.** Annotation supports the rendering modes, and each instance of the symbol in the app can use a different mode. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Annotate layers in the SF Symbols app to animate a custom symbol by layer.** Z-order sets the order in which variable color fills the layers, front to back or back to front, and layer groups let related layers move together. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Draw custom symbols as whole shapes, and test them with every animation preset.** Paths may not move as expected. Rather than cutting the gap between two people, draw the full shape plus an offset path annotated as an erase layer. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Create enclosures and badges with the component library instead of drawing them in.** The library keeps a custom symbol's common variants consistent with the included SF Symbols. (`sf-symbols`)
- **Assign negative side margins when a badge or similar element widens a custom symbol.** They keep a stack of folder symbols, some badged, optically aligned. Name each margin with its configuration, like left-margin-Regular-M. (`sf-symbols`)

## Interface icons (`icons`)

- **Design a recognizable, highly simplified icon around a familiar metaphor.** Too many details make an icon confusing or unreadable, and metaphors directly related to the action or content are recognized fastest. (`icons`)
- **Keep every interface icon consistent in size, detail, stroke weight, and perspective.** This holds when mixing custom and system icons. Depending on an icon's visual weight, adjust its dimensions so it looks consistent with the others. (`icons`)
- **Match an icon's weight to adjacent text, unless you want to emphasize one of them.** The same weight gives content a consistent appearance and level of emphasis. (`icons`)
- **Center icons optically, not geometrically, and build the offset into the asset as padding.** An asymmetric icon like download, heavier at the bottom, looks too low when centered geometrically. Centering the padded asset then centers the icon optically, and small adjustments make a big difference. (`icons`)
- **Don't make selected-state versions of icons in standard components.** In toolbars, tab bars, and buttons, the system updates the selected appearance automatically. (`icons`)
- **Use inclusive images.** Prefer gender-neutral human figures and images that are easy to recognize across cultures and languages, so the icons feel understandable and welcoming to everyone. (`icons`)
- **Include text in an icon only when it's essential to the meaning.** A character can be the most direct way to show text formatting, but localize any characters, and give an abstract passage of text a flipped version for right-to-left contexts. (`icons`)
- **Give every custom icon and custom symbol an alternative text label.** The label isn't visible, but it lets VoiceOver describe the interface for people with visual disabilities, which makes navigation easier. (`icons`, `sf-symbols`)
- **Don't draw replicas of Apple hardware.** Hardware designs change often and date your icons. If you must show Apple products, use the images in Apple Design Resources or the SF Symbols for Apple products. (`icons`)
- **Use the standard symbols for editing actions.** Cut is scissors, Copy document.on.document, Paste document.on.clipboard, Done and Save checkmark, Cancel and Close xmark, Delete trash, Undo and Redo arrow.uturn.backward and arrow.uturn.forward, Compose square.and.pencil, Duplicate plus.square.on.square, Rename pencil, Move to folder, Attach paperclip, Add plus, and More ellipsis. (`icons`)
- **Use the standard symbols for selection, search, and sharing.** Select is checkmark.circle and Deselect xmark, Search magnifyingglass, Find and its variants text.page.badge.magnifyingglass, Filter line.3.horizontal.decrease, Share and Export square.and.arrow.up, and Print printer. (`icons`)
- **Use the standard symbols for text formatting.** Bold, Italic, and Underline use bold, italic, and underline, Superscript and Subscript use textformat.superscript and textformat.subscript, and Align Left, Center, Justified, and Align Right use text.alignleft, text.aligncenter, text.justify, and text.alignright. (`icons`)
- **Use the standard symbols for accounts, ratings, layer order, and a few common items.** Account, User, and Profile use person.crop.circle, Like and Dislike hand.thumbsup and hand.thumbsdown, Bring to Front and Send to Back square.3.layers.3d.top.filled and its bottom.filled version, Bring Forward and Send Backward the square.2.layers.3d pair, and Alarm, Archive, and Calendar alarm, archivebox, and calendar. (`icons`)
- **(macOS) Consider a custom document icon for each custom document type the app handles.** It looks like paper with a folded top-right corner, which sets documents apart from apps even at small sizes. Without one, macOS composites the app icon and the extension, as Preview does for JPG files. (`icons`)
- **(macOS) Build a document icon from any mix of background fill, center image, and text.** The system layers, positions, and masks them onto the folded-corner shape, and a set of icons can separate related types, as Xcode does for projects, AR objects, and Swift files. (`icons`)
- **(macOS) Design simple document icon images that clearly communicate the document type.** Use uncomplicated shapes and a reduced palette of distinct colors, because a document icon can display as small as 16x16 px and must stay recognizable at every size. (`icons`)
- **(macOS) Consider one expressive background image to identify a document type.** Xcode and TextEdit use rich background images without a center image. (`icons`)
- **(macOS) Reduce complexity in a document icon's small sizes.** Details clear at large sizes blur when small: grid lines become fewer and thicker, aligned to the reduced pixel grid, then vanish at 16x16 px. (`icons`)
- **(macOS) Keep important content out of the top-right corner of a document icon's background fill.** The system masks the fill and draws the white folded corner over it. Provide background images at 16, 32, 128, 256, and 512 px, each at @1x and @2x. (`icons`)
- **(macOS) Consider a center image when a familiar object conveys the document's type or its tie to your app.** Make it simple, unambiguous, and recognizable at every size, at half the icon canvas: a 32x32 px icon takes a 16x16 px image. Provide 16, 32, 128, and 256 px, at @1x and @2x. (`icons`)
- **(macOS) Keep most of a center image within a margin of about 10% of its canvas.** The image should fill about 80%, with only optical-alignment overhangs reaching into the margin, so a 256x256 px canvas holds about 205x205 px. (`icons`)
- **(macOS) Replace an unfamiliar file extension with a short descriptive term.** A SceneKit scene file shows "scene" instead of "scn", and the system scales the term to fit and capitalizes every letter, so keep it short enough to read at small sizes. (`icons`)

## App icons (`app-icons`)

- **Express the app's purpose and personality in an icon people recognize at a glance.** It appears on the Home Screen and across the system, in search results, notifications, settings, and share sheets, and the system scales it down where needed. (`app-icons`)
- **Design the app icon in layers for the most control, though a flattened image also works.** In iOS, iPadOS, and macOS, a background layer and one or more foreground layers take on Liquid Glass highlights, refraction, and translucency, which adapt to icon size and can differ between system versions. (`app-icons`)
- **Craft foreground layers in your design tool, then assemble the icon in Icon Composer.** There you define the background, place the foreground layers, apply effects, annotate default, dark, and mono variants, preview across system versions, and export for Xcode. (`app-icons`)
- **Provide unmasked square layers, 1024x1024 px, and let the system mask them.** The system rounds the corners to match other rounded interface elements and the device bezel, and pre-masked layers weaken specular highlights and look jagged. (`app-icons`)
- **Keep primary content centered so masking doesn't truncate it.** Use the grids in the app icon production templates from Apple Design Resources. (`app-icons`)
- **Build the icon around one core idea with a minimal number of shapes.** Fine features look busy under system shadows and highlights and get lost at small sizes. Prefer a solid or gradient background, and don't fill the whole canvas. (`app-icons`)
- **Consider basing the design on filled, overlapping shapes.** Overlapping solid shapes in the foreground, especially with transparency and blurring, give an icon a sense of depth. (`app-icons`)
- **Give foreground layers clearly defined edges.** Soft or feathered edges keep the system-drawn highlights and shadows from looking their best. (`app-icons`)
- **Vary opacity in foreground layers for depth and liveliness.** The Photos icon splits its centerpiece into layers with translucent pieces. Import fully opaque layers and adjust transparency in Icon Composer to preview how it interacts with system effects. (`app-icons`)
- **Make the background stand out while emphasizing the foreground, and keep an imported background full-bleed and opaque.** A gradient must respond well to system lighting. Icon Composer supports solid colors and gradients, so custom background images are rarely needed. (`app-icons`)
- **Prefer vector layers (SVG or PDF) with text converted to outlines, and PNG for mesh gradients and raster art.** Vectors stay crisp at any size and PNG is lossless. App icons support sRGB, Gray Gamma 2.2, and Display P3 color spaces. (`app-icons`)
- **Keep the icon visually consistent across every platform the app supports.** People find the app faster wherever it appears and don't mistake it for several different apps. (`app-icons`)
- **Include text only when it's essential to the experience or brand.** Icon text doesn't support accessibility or localization, is often too small, and repeats the nearby app name. A mnemonic letter can help, but skip words like Watch, Play, or New. (`app-icons`)
- **Prefer illustrations to photos, and never replicate standard UI components or screenshots.** Photo detail fails at small sizes, across appearances, and when split into layers. Avoid extremely thin lines and sharp corners, which lose crispness when small. (`app-icons`)
- **Never use replicas of Apple hardware products in an app icon.** Apple products are copyrighted and can't be reproduced in app icons. (`app-icons`)
- **Let the system draw highlights, shadows, blurs, and other effects.** Custom specular highlights, drop shadows between layers, bevels, blurs, and glows interfere with the system's effects, and they're static while the system's are dynamic. Test any you add in Icon Composer, Device Hub, or on a device. (`app-icons`)
- **Group layers when effects should apply to several at once.** System effects usually apply per layer, and for a group, Icon Composer offers extra Liquid Glass options like specular highlights, refraction, and translucency. (`app-icons`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS, macOS) Design for every appearance people can choose: default, dark, clear, and tinted.** People pick a Home Screen icon appearance, for example to suit their wallpaper. Clear and tinted come in light and dark, making six variants, and the system generates any you don't provide. (`app-icons`)
- **Keep core features identical across the default, dark, clear, and tinted appearances.** Swapping elements in and out of variants makes the app harder to find when people switch appearances. (`app-icons`)
- **Design dark and tinted icons that feel at home beside system app icons and widgets.** You can keep the default palette, but dark icons are more subdued and clear and tinted ones even more, and a great icon stays visible, legible, and recognizable in every variant. (`app-icons`)
- **Derive the dark icon from the light one.** Use complementary colors that reflect the default design, avoid excessively bright images, and favor color backgrounds, which give the greatest contrast in dark icons. (`app-icons`)
- **(iOS, iPadOS) Consider offering alternate app icons, each closely tied to your content.** People choose one in the app's settings, like a sports app's team icons. None should look like another app, and each needs its own dark, clear, and tinted variants and is subject to app review. (`app-icons`)
